# See my readiness

## PROBLEM

"Am I ready for this course?" is the question every student carries all term, and not one of them can answer it.

What they have instead are proxies: how many lectures they attended, whether their folder feels full, how anxious they feel this week. None of these correspond to whether they can do what the course demands.

The strange part is that both halves of the real answer already exist and are never put side by side. The course declared what it expects, in objectives nobody reads after week one. The student has been generating evidence about what they can actually do all term, in the form of summaries written and practice taken. Nothing brings the expectation and the evidence together.

So gaps stay invisible until they are graded. A student can work hard all semester on the parts they already enjoy and never notice the objective they have no material for and have never once tested themselves on.

## USER

A student partway through a term deciding what to study next — and the same student in the week before an exam, deciding where to spend the little time that remains.

## OUTCOME

The student sees, objective by objective, what the course expects and what they have actually done about it: whether material exists for it, whether they have tested themselves on it, and how that went.

"Am I behind?" becomes a question with an answer. More usefully, "what should I do next?" gets a specific one instead of a vague dread.

It also makes a term's accumulated effort visible. Every session captured and every practice completed moves something the student can see — which is what keeps the effort worth making in the weeks when no exam is close enough to supply the motivation.

## SUCCESS

- Students open readiness during the term, not only in the days before an exam.
- Students act on it: what it flags as thin is what they capture or practise next.
- Objectives that begin the term with no evidence behind them acquire some by the end.
- It matches reality. If readiness says a student is ready and the exam disagrees, the feature is worse than useless — it replaced a vague accurate anxiety with a precise false comfort.

## LIMITS

- Readiness reports evidence, never a predicted grade. It says what the student has and has not done, not how they will score.
- It must be honest about thin evidence. A gap has to look like a gap, even when the student has been working hard elsewhere.
- Untouched objectives are the most important thing it shows, and must not be crowded out by the areas the student has already invested in. The whole point is to surface what is being avoided.
- It depends on objectives existing. Where there are none, it must say so and point at fixing that, rather than presenting a confident empty state.
- It is a study aid for one student. It is not a report to anyone else, and nothing in its design should assume a future reader other than them.

## NOT NOW

- Sharing readiness with instructors, institutions, or parents
- Predicted grades or exam-score estimates
- Comparison against other students or cohort averages
- Planning the student's week or scheduling their study time
