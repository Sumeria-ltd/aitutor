# Check my summary

## PROBLEM

A student can read a slide deck through and finish it feeling they understood it. That feeling is famously unreliable. Recognition is not recall, and rereading reliably produces confidence without producing competence.

The only way to find out what a student actually holds is for them to produce it from memory and compare it against the source. Almost none of them do, and the reason is simple: there is nobody to compare against. Writing a summary and then grading it yourself requires already knowing the thing you are trying to discover you don't know. A student cannot mark their own blind spots, because they are blind spots.

So the feeling substitutes for the fact, and the difference between them is discovered in the exam.

## USER

A student who has just attended or reviewed a session and believes they understood it.

## OUTCOME

The student writes what they took from a session in their own words and gets back a specific account of what they got right, what they left out, and what they have subtly wrong.

The effortful and genuinely valuable act — producing understanding from memory rather than recognizing it on a slide — finally has a response. That response is what makes it worth doing a second time.

This is the moment the product earns its keep. The student does real cognitive work and, within seconds, sees exactly where it landed. It converts studying from something with delayed and invisible payoff into something with an immediate scoreboard.

## SUCCESS

- A student who checks one summary goes on to check more. This is the single strongest signal that the payoff is real, and the clearest evidence the whole product thesis holds.
- Feedback names specific missing content rather than offering generic encouragement. A student should be able to act on it within the next ten minutes.
- Students revise and resubmit after feedback, rather than reading it and closing the tab.
- Over a term, summaries get written closer to the lecture, as the habit takes hold.

## LIMITS

- The student writes first. Nothing is shown beforehand that would let them assemble a summary without recalling one.
- The AI never writes the summary, never offers to, and never shows a model answer before the student has committed to theirs.
- Feedback is measured against the session's own material, not against general knowledge of the subject. A student is not wrong for omitting something their professor never taught.
- Feedback must be specific and located: what was missed, and where in the session it lives.
- Being wrong is the entire point. Tone must make an incorrect summary feel like useful information rather than a failure — the moment it feels like judgment, students start writing summaries designed to score well instead of honest ones, and the feature is dead.

## NOT NOW

- Grades, scores, or marks attached to a summary
- Any comparison against other students
- Checking one summary across several sessions at once
- Rewriting or improving the student's own text for them
