# Intent 0006 — Check my summary

Derived from PRD requirement 0006, rank 6.

PROBLEM:  A learner can read a slide deck through and finish it feeling they understood it.
          That feeling is famously unreliable. Recognition is not recall, and rereading
          reliably produces confidence without producing competence.
          The only way to find out what a learner actually holds is for them to produce it
          from memory and compare it against the source. Almost none of them do, and the
          reason is simple: there is nobody to compare against. Writing a summary and then
          grading it yourself requires already knowing the thing you are trying to discover
          you don't know. A learner cannot mark their own blind spots, because they are blind
          spots. So the feeling substitutes for the fact, and the difference between them is
          discovered in the exam.

USER:     A learner who has just attended or reviewed a session and believes they understood
          it.

OUTCOME:  The learner writes what they took from a session in their own words and gets back a
          specific account of what they got right, what they left out, and what they have
          subtly wrong. The effortful and genuinely valuable act — producing understanding
          from memory rather than recognizing it on a slide — finally has a response, and that
          response is what makes it worth doing a second time. This is the moment the product
          earns its keep: the learner does real cognitive work and, within seconds, sees
          exactly where it landed.

SUCCESS:  Within one week of this existing, five summaries are submitted that each
          deliberately omit a point the session covers. Count the responses naming that
          specific omission and where in the session it lives: five. Then count the learners
          who, having checked one summary, check a second inside the same week: at least two of
          five. The second number is the one that matters; it is the clearest evidence the
          product's central bet holds.

LIMITS:   The learner writes first. Nothing is shown beforehand that would let them assemble a
          summary without recalling one. The product never writes the summary, never offers
          to, and never shows a model answer before the learner has committed to theirs.
          Feedback is measured against the session's own material, not against general
          knowledge of the subject — a learner is not wrong for omitting something their
          instructor never taught. Feedback must be specific and located: what was missed, and
          where in the session it lives. Being wrong is the entire point, so tone must make an
          incorrect summary feel like useful information rather than a failure; the moment it
          feels like judgment, learners start writing summaries designed to score well instead
          of honest ones, and the requirement is dead.

NOT NOW:  Grades, scores, or marks attached to a summary. Any comparison against other
          learners. Checking one summary across several sessions at once. Rewriting or
          improving the learner's own text for them.

## Open questions
- Feedback must be specific without being a model answer the learner could have copied. Where
  exactly is that line? "You did not mention entropy" is specific and safe; a paragraph
  explaining entropy is the summary written for them.
- What happens when a learner's summary is right about something the session got wrong? Measuring
  against the session's own material is the stated limit, which means the product would mark a
  correct learner as mistaken.
- Is a revision counted as a second check for the success measure above, or only a new session's
  summary? The two behaviours mean different things and the measure currently does not
  distinguish them.
